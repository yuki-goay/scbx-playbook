New 2.1 Why we're reimagining delivery

Product teams often work from separate assumptions about users and goals. Each function has its own read on the problem. That misalignment surfaces as rework, delayed timelines and designs that do not hold up to scrutiny. The design approach in Agentic PDLC keeps evidence connected across every phase, so the people working with it can trust it and the whole team designs from the same picture.

The design capabilities are organised across three phases: Discover, Define, and Design. Each phase has a defined set of capabilities, supported by agents, that produce the artefacts the next phase depends on.

Find out more about capabilities that support the design process

AG-01 Research planner

Helps the team get ready before talking to anyone. It turns the vision, objectives and early stakeholder notes into a list of assumptions, a research plan, interview guides and screeners, so fieldwork starts with clear questions instead of guesses. It prepares the ground but does not run the research itself, and it updates the plan whenever new conversations change what we need to ask.

  • Input: Vision and objectives, stakeholder and expert interview notes, briefs and earlier work.
  • Output: Assumptions log, research plan, interview guides and screeners.

AG-02 Insights synthesizer

Makes sense of what users told us. Once a person has checked the raw transcripts, notes and recordings, it files and tags them as evidence, then drafts the insights, personas, blueprints and test findings, each traceable to where it came from. It also helps plan, log and analyse tests, and keeps the evidence repository up to date.

  • Input: checked and tagged evidence, personas, pain points, prioritised features, the to-be blueprint, principles, prototypes and pass criteria.
  • Output: tagged evidence, insights, personas, blueprints and test findings.

AG-03 Experience designer

Turns decisions into something people can see and try. It drafts the structure of the experience, the design language and the detailed screens, builds a working concept prototype from the approved design system, and checks the designs against our principles and standards.

  • Input: the approved blueprint and features, validated gaps, experience principles, and the current structure and design language.
  • Output: the experience structure, design language, detailed screens and check results, plus the coded prototype, which goes back for testing.

Deciding when an agent should do the work

Someone has asked: “Can the agent do this?” The answer is rarely obvious. Agents accelerate the right kind of work and complicate the wrong kind.

The difference usually comes down to what the step asks for. Mechanical steps, like tagging evidence or logging session feedback follow rules that can be written down. An agent can do them faster than a person, and a person can check the result at a glance. Judgment steps, like framing the problem, deciding what goes forward or choosing which underserved jobs matter, depend on context, trade-offs and accountability. An agent can prepare the ground for these, but it should not own the decision.

Most steps sit in between. In mixed work, the agent drafts and the human decides.

Three questions help settle it quickly:

  1. Can the output be checked against evidence? If a person can verify it in a click, the agent can take it on. If the only test is “does this feel right?”, a person should lead.
  2. What does a wrong answer cost? A mislabelled tag is cheap to fix. A mis-framed problem steers every step after it.
  3. Does the step need someone to stand behind it? Sign-offs, priorities and trade-offs need an owner, and that owner should be human.

Let the agent handle the mechanical portion and keep the human on the part that needs a call, such as deciding which segments matter. This is also where handoff design matters most, because each split creates a point where work passes between human and agent, and the person needs to see what was done and be able to step back in.

Handoff and keeping the evidence trail

In an agentic process, work changes hands far more often: between phases, between people, and between people and agents. Each crossing is a place where context can go missing, such as the reason behind a decision or why a direction was ruled out. A person can often fill the gap from memory or a hallway conversation. An agent cannot. It only knows what was written down, so a missing rationale becomes a confident wrong assumption that every later step builds on.

This is why the evidence trail is a core capability rather than administrative work. Recording decisions and their rationale as the work happens lets an agent pick up where a person left off, lets a person verify what an agent did, and lets a new team member or stakeholder see why a direction was chosen without relying on anyone’s recall. It is also what makes the principle of visible evidence real: users can only check an agent’s work if the trail exists.

Agents are never finished, they are tuned

No agent will fit every team on day one. Designers work differently, evidence lives in different places, and what counts as a “good enough” first pass varies from person to person. So the agent design process should be iterative by default: ship the simplest version, watch how people use it, and adjust.

The 11 capabilities across the agentic design process:

Discover

The Discover phase establishes shared understanding of the problem before any solution work begins. The goal is not to produce volume, but to produce a clear and evidenced problem statement that the whole team can work from.

  • Align and plan
  • Gather and organise evidence
  • Draw out insights and needs
  • Map the current state
  • Frame the problem

Define

The Define phase converts discovery evidence into a validated solution direction. Concepts are generated, prioritised and tested before detailed design begins.

  • Ideate and prioritise
  • Test concepts

Design

The Design phase produces the detailed, tested solution ready for handoff. It covers information architecture, design language, detailed screens and design quality checks.

  • Structure the experience
  • Define and maintain the design language
  • Design the detailed screens
  • Test usability
